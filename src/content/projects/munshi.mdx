## The problem

At the end of the night, a restaurant owner in Pakistan often can't say what was
actually sold. Orders were taken in one system, stock was tracked in a paper
register, and salaries were worked out by hand at the end of the month. Every
report meant collecting numbers from three places, and a short cash drawer was
found weeks later, if at all.

The software on offer didn't fit how these restaurants work. Most point-of-sale
systems are built for steady internet, English menus and card payments. Here,
the connection drops in the middle of the dinner rush, menus are written in
Urdu, regulars and suppliers run on _khata_ (credit kept in a notebook), and the
kitchen works from paper tickets.

So the brief was simple to say and hard to build: one app that runs the whole
restaurant, keeps taking orders when the internet is down, prints on the cheap
thermal printers restaurants already own, and reads in Urdu.

## RFC, the first customer

RFC serves karahi, BBQ, burgers and pizza on Main Faisalabad–Lahore Road in
Manawala. It was the first restaurant to run on Munshi, and it still does.

- **10,500+** orders taken on Munshi
- **163** orders on its busiest day
- **9** staff on attendance and payroll
- Running on Munshi since **September 2025**

**Switching over.** Munshi was built alongside RFC's own counter and kitchen, so
every screen was tested in a real rush before it shipped. Staff moved over one
area at a time: the counter first, then the kitchen tickets, then purchases and
payroll. The restaurant never had to stop and switch everything at once.

**Now.** Every order, ticket, purchase and shift lives in one place. The drawer
is counted every night, so a shortage shows up the same day instead of weeks
later. _Khata_ balances for regulars and suppliers update themselves as bills
and payments go in. Salaries come straight out of the attendance the staff
already mark, so nobody works out payroll by hand.

## How it works

### What people use

- **Orders:** dine-in, takeaway and delivery, plus an online ordering page for
  each restaurant. Bills, payments and kitchen tickets, including tickets for
  changes to an order.
- **Kitchen:** a live display that updates on every screen as orders change.
- **Stock:** products, variants and recipes, so selling a dish takes its
  ingredients out of stock. Intake, adjustments and waste.
- **Staff:** roles the owner designs, attendance, and payroll worked out from
  it.
- **Cash and khata:** opening and closing the drawer, expenses, and running
  balances for regulars and suppliers.
- **Branches and reports:** each outlet runs on its own. Managers see theirs,
  owners see all of them, with sales and menu reports across branches.

### Under the hood

One Next.js app on PostgreSQL through Prisma, with a small realtime server
beside it (Socket.IO) that keeps the counter, kitchen and admin screens in sync
and runs the scheduled jobs.

- Every restaurant lives in one database and gets its own subdomain.
- Staff sign in with an outlet code, a staff code and a PIN. Permissions are set
  per role and per outlet.
- Bills, kitchen tickets and tokens print through the browser, sized for 80mm
  thermal paper.
- It installs like an app and keeps working offline. That part has its own story
  [below](#the-hard-part).

## Decisions

**All restaurants share one database.** Munshi first ran each customer on its
own server and database. Onboarding a restaurant was a manual job of about three
days, and every update had to be rolled out once per customer. I moved everyone
onto one shared database before the third customer arrived, while the move was
still cheap. Now adding a restaurant is a single step, and one migration updates
everyone. The cost: keeping one restaurant's data away from another's is the
code's job, not the database's. That's why a suite of 19 test files exists only
to try to reach one restaurant's data from another.

**Printing goes through the browser.** Bills, kitchen tickets and tokens are
laid out in the browser and sent to whatever printer the computer already has,
so there's nothing to install at the restaurant. The receipt is built from order
data already on the device, which means printing keeps working when the internet
is down. It also solved Urdu for free: the browser draws the text, so Urdu
prints like any other script, which is hard with raw printer commands. The cost:
tickets can't yet be routed automatically to a separate kitchen printer. I'll
add direct printer commands when a restaurant actually needs that.

**Each restaurant gets its own subdomain.** Browsers tie sign-in cookies, the
offline cache and the installed app to a domain, not a path. Two restaurants on
one address would share a cookie jar and install the same app icon. A subdomain
each keeps them apart, even on a shared device. The server works out the
restaurant from the address itself and ignores any restaurant the browser claims
to be. Branches share their restaurant's subdomain; staff reach their outlet
through the code they sign in with.

**Urdu names are data, not translation.** A restaurant's Urdu menu isn't a
translation of its English one: <span lang='ur'>چکن کڑاہی</span> is simply the
dish's name. So each dish and category carries its own Urdu name, and each
restaurant picks English, Urdu or both, with right-to-left text only where Urdu
appears. It took one database column instead of a translation framework. The
cost: buttons and labels are still in English, and a fully right-to-left
interface waits until a restaurant needs it.

## The hard part

**Taking orders with the internet down.** Picture a full night, and the
connection drops. The cashier has to keep ringing up orders and the kitchen has
to keep getting tickets. When the internet comes back, every one of those orders
has to reach the server exactly once: none lost, none counted twice.

At first the app opened offline and let a waiter build a whole order, then
failed at the moment of saving, with no warning. That was worse than an outage,
because it lied until the money was on the counter.

The fix is an outbox on the device. An order taken offline is saved locally with
a temporary number like #TMP-4F2A, its bill and kitchen ticket print straight
away, and a background loop sends the backlog whenever the server can be
reached. When the order lands, its card quietly becomes #014.

The traps were in the details:

- **The browser said we were online.** `navigator.onLine` only knows the Wi-Fi
  is up. At RFC the router stayed connected with a dead line behind it, so the
  app now checks whether the server itself answers.
- **The cache was too helpful.** The service worker's default setup cached every
  API response for 24 hours, so a stale answer could make the app look online,
  and staff could see day-old data. API calls now always go to the network.
- **Retries must not double orders.** Every queued write carries an idempotency
  key, so the server recognises a resend and does the work once. Some refusals
  really mean "already done", like cancelling an order that's already cancelled,
  and count as success.
- **Not every failure is the same.** No network or an expired sign-in pauses the
  queue, a server error backs off and retries, and a real conflict is parked for
  a person to fix. A background sync never throws a waiter onto the login screen
  in the middle of service.
- **Two tabs, one queue.** A browser lock stops two tabs from sending the same
  orders, and editing an order that hasn't synced yet rewrites it in the queue
  instead of queueing behind it.

What it taught me: offline isn't a feature you add at the end. It changes what
every write has to promise.

**Another one, briefly.** One day a manager couldn't sign in, and nothing
anywhere said why. Granting their role one more permission had pushed the
sign-in token past 4 KB, and browsers silently drop a cookie that large. I cut
the token from about 4 KB to 3.4 KB and added a check that warns long before the
limit.

## Outcome so far

RFC has run on Munshi since September 2025, and the numbers above are its own.
Moving every restaurant onto one database changed what growth costs: adding a
new restaurant went from about three days of manual setup to under five minutes.

What I'd change: I'd test offline mode on real restaurant hardware sooner. Half
of its scenarios have so far only been tested in the browser. And I'd add CI
instead of relying on the check script I run before each deploy.

What's next: the restaurant system is the first of a family of management
systems built on the same foundation. Grocery management comes next.
